# Thank the Host

The thank-you is the most skipped and cheapest step in the whole
chain, and it is the step that generates invitations. Venues host many
authors; they remember the ones who made them look good and said so
specifically. This chapter is small: three messages and one habit.

## Message one: the thank-you, inside 48 hours

Handwritten beats email, but email within two days beats handwriting
that arrives next month. Whichever you choose, it is short and
specific:

- name at least two staff members and the exact thing they did:
  "Rosa, the projector was already warm when I got there";
- one honest number: nineteen guests, six books sold, four newsletter
  signups -- hosts report attendance upward, and real numbers help
  them defend your slot next season;
- one offer before they must ask: "For next year, I'd gladly run the
  same session again, or a beginners' version";

The store version of numbers: "fifteen copies through your till --
thank you for ringing them up so graciously." Same warmth, different
rail.

## Message two: the public thanks, a day or two later

One social post, once, tagged and grateful:

> Yesterday's session at [VENUE] drew a full room -- thanks to [NAME]
> and the staff for a warm welcome, and to everyone who asked the
> stubborn questions. Next stop: [what's next, if you know].

Tag the venue's actual account, not the city at large. Libraries and
independent stores both repost gratitude that makes them look
generous; that repost is your next pitch's cover letter. Keep the post
clear of sales numbers -- gratitude publicly, commerce privately.

## Message three: the recap, only when it helps

Bookstore managers and program coordinators keep metrics. One email,
three lines, a week later:

> Hi Rosa -- quick recap from Thursday: 19 attendees, 6 copies sold,
  4 signups for my free note. Happy to send my one-page kit summary
  for next year's planning if useful.

Send it when the venue tracks numbers. Skip it for a tiny branch
visit where two guests felt like a blessing, not a metric -- a hand
note covers that case better.

## The one habit: the visit file

Everything about a venue ends up in one small file (a folder of
templates, a sheet of contacts -- see
[Make It Repeatable](07-make-it-repeatable.md) for the layout). The
thank-you step adds three lines to it:

- who made it happen (names, spelled right),
- what the room's setup actually was (outlet location, table count,
  lighting mood),
- one line on how the session felt to you, honestly.

Visitors improve between visits or do not. The difference is a file
nobody sees.

## When the thank-you becomes the next yes

- End every thank-you with a door open, not a pitch: "If a second
  branch would ever be interested, happy to send the same poster."
- Deliver anything you promised in front of the room: the promised
  handout to someone who asked, the promised intro to another local
  author, the promised photos.
- Never ask for the next date inside the thank-you. Ask inside the
  visit file review a month later, when the warm feeling arrives
  rather than the calendar.
