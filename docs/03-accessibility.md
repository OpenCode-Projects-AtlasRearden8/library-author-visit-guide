# Accessibility Without a Committee

Accessibility at a small author event is mostly obeying a few obvious
rules that cost nothing. Guests do not need a workshop on their needs;
they need text they can read, speech they can hear, and a demonstration
that does not require perfect eyes. This chapter is the whole
rulebook: large print, plain English, and the bits of hearing and
mobility that make or break an evening.

## Large print: everything, not just the slides

If people strain, they stop. Apply the same rules everywhere:

- **18-point minimum for anything projected or posted.** Too-small
  projected text is the most common avoidable failure at small author
  events; signage and slides included.
- **14-point minimum for handouts**, 18 if the handout is *the* content
  (a worksheet, a checklist, the read-aloud page).
- **High contrast, always.** Dark ink on light paper beats color
  backgrounds. Never light gray on white.
- **One idea per line, short lines, left-aligned.** Justified text
  gapes and squeezes unpredictably; left-aligned lines never do.
- **Sans-serif for headings and slides;** a comfortable serif for body
  pages is fine, but never script or decorative fonts anywhere.
- **Print counts, even for the demo.** When the projector fails you
  will hand out printed screenshots instead -- and those pages follow
  these rules already.

## Plain English: the rulebook that made the book

The manual's authoring rule was "explain AI like a friend would." Use
it in the room:

- **Short sentences, short paragraphs, one thought each.** Write your
  speech, then cut a fifth of the words; read the remainder aloud
  into your phone's voice recorder and cut where you stumble.
- **Define every term when you first say it.** "Tokens -- roughly, the
  chunks words get cut into -- cost pennies." Keep the
  [glossary](08-glossary.md) printed for your own reference and hand
  guests a copy when a term lands on them cold.
- **Name the jargon, then retire it.** "What most people call prompt
  engineering is really just clear instructions." Then never say the
  jargon word again without a plain-English neighbor.
- **Numbers in real-life units,** never in percentages of things
  nobody tracks: "about the price of a coffee," not "a 63% reduction."
- **Never condescend.** Plain English is respect, not simplification:
  explain *why* in the same breath as *what*.

## Hearing: small habits, huge difference

- **Face the room and speak to the back row,** not your laptop.
- **Repeat every question** before answering -- the standard elsewhere
  in this manual, but it belongs in the hearing rules too.
- **Use the microphone if it exists.** Rooms where "you won't need it"
  almost always need it.
- **Avoid talking while facing the screen.** Turn and deliver, then
  glance back.
- **Write the contact line on paper or a slide,** not spoken alone:
  "email for my free monthly note" said once is gone; written once
  survives.

## Sight: never say "as you can see here"

Describe what a sight-impaired guest would otherwise miss:

- narrate your demo: what is on the screen, in words;
- describe the props rather than pointing: "on the left of the table,
  the blue folder";
- handouts get offered individually, not left on a distant stack;
- leave room lights up enough for print and faces -- projector-dark
  rooms exclude a third of the room.

## Body and energy: design for the tired hour

- **Everything in reach.** Demo laptop, books, handouts, and card
  reader live on one table; no ducking under AV carts.
- **Sit if you need to.** A seated welcome and a seated demo read fine
  -- chairs and a table are enough.
- **Move chairs, then start.** First five minutes invite people to
  shift out of lecture rows into a shallow arc; hearing improves,
  sightlines improve, and small talk up front gets easier rather than
  harder for everyone.
- **"Access needs welcome."** Put one plain sentence in every event
  listing you control: "Access needs welcome -- just ask." Do not
  demand accommodation types on a form; quietly greeting the aisle
  seat request on the night is what actually helps.

## The pre-event accessibility pass

Twenty minutes, at home, before you pack the car:

- [ ] Every printed page is 14-point-plus; handouts 16-18.
- [ ] Any slide text is 18-point-plus and high contrast.
- [ ] All jargon in your talk has a spoken gloss.
- [ ] Demo narration covers what the screen shows.
- [ ] You repeat questions out of habit, no thinking needed.
- [ ] Read-aloud copy is a marked-up paper sheet, not a phone.
- [ ] One sentence invites access needs on the listing.

Small rooms fail at accessibility by accident, not intention. These
rules keep you on the kind side of that line, and guests always notice.
