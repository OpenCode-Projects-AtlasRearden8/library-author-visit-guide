# Pitch the Visit

Most rejection in local event booking is not about the author. It is
about unsolved problems on the venue's side: an empty slot, a staff
member who has to manage everything, A/V equipment nobody maintains,
and guests who linger past closing. A good pitch solves
those problems before they are asked about. This chapter shows how.

## Understand what each venue is actually buying

You are not selling a book. You are offering staff a *solution*: a full
hour of genuine value for their community that costs them nothing.

| Venue | What the staff care about | What makes them say yes |
|---|---|---|
| **Public library** | Programming calendars with gaps; attendance metrics; free, neutral, welcoming events | A free event that draws people, needs no library funds, and is clearly laid out and low-maintenance on the day of |
| **Independent bookstore** | Foot traffic; margin on what actually sells | An event that brings people who buy, run by an author who arrives early, brings their own table kit, and says thanks afterward |
| **Community center / club** | A calendar members actually use; activities for regulars | A practical, friendly session that fits an existing slot -- crafters, hobbyists, retirees, writers' groups -- with no fee or membership needed |

The same fifty-minute session works at all three. What changes is the
first two lines of your pitch: the benefit you lead with. Lead with the
thing the specific venue cares about most, shown in the table above.

## The yes factors: put them in every pitch

Pitch emails fail when they describe the author ("I have such a unique
voice") and succeed when they remove the venue's work. Say plainly:

- **Free for you.** There is no speaker fee. There are no equipment
  costs. There is nothing to invoice.
- **I bring everything.** Own laptop and adapters, own extension cord,
  own signage and book float. Staff handle nothing beyond unlocking the room.
- **A complete sixty-minute guest experience, fifty of it on the
  program.** Say the shape so they can picture it: an author reading,
  questions, and a short, friendly demonstration of the tools behind
  the book.
- **A real audience.** Name who it is for: "for local writers, early
  retirees, or anyone curious about AI but tired of the hype" beats any
  phrase that includes the word *platform*.
- **Graceful exit built in.** Mention you start and end on time and
  will keep the room clear of lingering equipment well before closing.

That is it. Six sentences of body *can* carry all five bullet groups if
you cut adjectives. The [pitch letter template](../templates/pitch-letter-template.md) shows the exact shape.

## Who to contact, and when

- **Library:** programming librarian, "adult services," or whoever runs
  the events calendar. Their email is on the branch site; failing that,
  call and ask, plainly: "Who schedules author events, and would they
  prefer email or a visit?"
- **Bookstore:** owner or events manager (indie stores) and community
  outreach staff (larger chains). Independent stores respond better in
  person, mid-afternoon, never at rush.
- **Community center:** for a community center or a club, try the
  program coordinator or the front desk, whichever shoulders the
  calendar.

Timing rules of thumb: pitch **8-10 weeks** before you want the date
(library calendars are made two months out), confirm **2 weeks** out,
and never pitch your biggest-dream venue first. Do the rehearsals at a
small branch or a club you belong to first.

## Send, wait, follow up -- the ladder

1. **Day 0:** one short pitch email. No attachments. Subject line with
   the venue and the word *free*.
2. **Day 7-10:** one-nudge follow-up, three sentences long, in the same
   email thread. "Do you need anything else so I can make a decision?"
   It reads friendlier than it sounds.
3. **Day 14:** make it easy to say yes. Offer a phone call or a short visit if that is
   easier than email.
4. **After a no:** thank them anyway and ask a single favor: "Any other
   branch or coordinator you'd suggest?" Nos routinely become other
   venues' yeses.

## Handling the three answers

- **Yes:** respond the same day with the date in bold, your arrival
  time, your needs (outlet, chair count, table), and one copy of the
  [day-of checklist](../templates/day-of-checklist.md) summary so they
  see how much you have handled. Pin the date down the same day while
  the enthusiasm is live; slots move quietly without a rope around
  them.
- **Not now:** ask when their next programming window opens. Pin it in
  your calendar. Pitch again on that day, not before.
- **No:** stay warm, ask for a referral, and book the next venue that
  same day so the no doesn't live in your head rent-free.

## A note for authors of how-to and AI books

Staff sometimes hesitate at anything that sounds like "tech demo" --
they fear Internet problems and jargon. Defuse it in the pitch itself:

> The demonstration is deliberately simple: I sign nothing, log into
> nothing, and the key screenshots are printed. If the Wi-Fi
  misbehaves, the session is identical.

Ending public fears before they start is the cheapest thing in this
whole chapter.
