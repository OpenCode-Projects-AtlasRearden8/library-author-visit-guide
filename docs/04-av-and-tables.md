# A/V and Tables Basics

Small venues have small A/V: an HDMI port of uncertain temper, a
microphone with a dead battery, one table of the wrong height. The local
author who never gets burned is not lucky -- they own a two-hundred-
dollar total kit and arrive thirty minutes early. This chapter is the
kit and the checklist.

## The A/V truce: assume nothing works

Plan the evening so that every piece of borrowed technology can fail
without a single guest noticing more than a ripple:

- **Your own laptop**, charged, with auto-updates and sleep disabled
  for the night. Updates steal projectors' first impression.
- **Every adapter you could need:** HDMI-to-USB-C, HDMI-to-USB-A,
  plus the plain HDMI-to-HDMI cable. Venues never have the one you
  lack; this rule has no known counterexample.
- **The presentation saved in three places:** laptop drive, one USB
  stick, and your phone. Screenshots of the tool demo live on the stick
  too, in case everything else dies.
- **Printed pages of everything essential:** your read-aloud copy, the
  demo screenshots, and one paper slide outline. Paper is the only
  medium that has never failed anyone.
- **A ten-foot extension cord** and a power strip. Meeting-room outlets
  are placed by someone who hates events.

## Three questions for the host, asked a week ahead

Send these with your confirmation, three lines, plainly:

1. "What does your projector or screen take -- HDMI, USB-C, or
   cast-only?" (If cast-only, say you will use screenshots on a stick
   and arrive early to test.)
2. "Is there a microphone worth using, or do we talk over the room?"
3. "Who unlocks the room and who locks up -- and what time?"

Answers go in your visit file (chapter 7 explains the file). The
[day-of checklist](../templates/day-of-checklist.md) turns them into a
timeline.

## The half-hour before guests arrive

Arrive thirty minutes early. Then:

- [ ] Plug in everything once, in front of the room, and stand at
      speaking distance to see it from the back corner.
- [ ] Play your first slide and the demo's opening screenshot. If text
      is unreadable from the back, it is unreadable, full stop: bump
      the size or drop the slide.
- [ ] Find the light switches. Bring the room up bright enough for
      faces and print; see [Accessibility](03-accessibility.md).
- [ ] Lay the book table now, not at minute five of Q&A.
- [ ] Fill your water, find the restroom, and load the door easel.

## Tables: the unglamorous half of the evening

The table is where the visit turns into sales and signups, so the
table deserves engineering:

- **One table, cloth first.** A cheap plain or dark fabric cloth makes
  any folding table look deliberate and makes covers pop. Bring your
  own; venues scrounge.
- **Back-facing row display.** Books stand upright and face guests in
  one neat row, spine faces on a riser if you have one, nothing stacked
  tall; a scanning guest sees all titles from five feet out.
- **Signage in order.** One large-letter sign: title, price, which
  payment methods work. One small sign: "Take a handout -- free." Price
  on the book itself only if the venue's policy loves stickers.
- **Everything within a guest's reach:** card reader, cash box with
  small bills, pen for signing, extra bookmarks, and your signup sheet
  (a paper sheet for the newsletter, with the QR fallback).
- **Cables are taped or routed along an edge.** Guests trip; bad
  evenings are made of trips.
- **Height matters.** Fold-up tables at 29-30 inches are fine, but
  standing at one corner of the table instead of behind it -- the
  barista position -- changes the entire mood of buying. Stay out from
  behind the table whenever you can.

## Payment without friction, stated plainly

- **Two ways to pay, both quick:** a card reader app from your phone
  and a small cash box with change. Ten one-dollar bills and ten
  quarters do not feel like much until the first evening they are.
- **Test the reader at home** with a $0.10 sale to your own card
  beforehand. The first transaction of the night is never the one to
  debug live.
- **Print the price.** Guests should see the price without asking; the
  ask is where hesitation lives.
- **Know your tax obligation in your state,** now, before the first
  sale -- one phone call or one page on your state's revenue site. This
  manual is not tax advice; the point is only that a Tuesday night sale
  is real income with real, usually small, paperwork.

## When it all goes sideways

Projector dies: hand out the printed screenshots and narrate. Wi-Fi
dies: run the airplane-mode demo. Microphone dies: face the room,
repeat every question, and move closer, slower. Nobody remembers what
failed at a good event; everybody remembers the author who stayed
pleasant when it did.
