# Start Here

Friendly, free author visits run on a promise: one good hour that leaves
the audience glad they came and the staff glad they said yes. This manual
shows you how to make and keep that promise -- how to pitch a library,
bookstore, or community center so the answer is yes, how to plan a
fifty-minute session, how to set up an accessible room and a dignified
book table, and how to turn visit number one into a kit that makes visit
number two almost free.

It is written by Dylan Asdale, a nurse turned AI author, whose
**The Massive AI Manual** explains AI "like a friend would": plain
English, step by step, never condescending. That voice is the whole
method. The same rules that make the books readable -- warm, concrete,
no showing off -- are the rules that make a room full of strangers
trust you on a Tuesday evening.

## Who this is for

Any local author who wants to meet real readers in real rooms:

- self-published and small-press authors, including first-timers with
  one book and no "platform"
- authors whose books are about tools (like AI guides) and who want to
  demo those tools without turning the evening into a tech webinar
- authors with day jobs and low energy budgets, who need a plan with
  margins and shortlists, not a performance-industry playbook
- any author who would rather be invited back than be impressive once

Nothing here requires a budget. The visit itself should be free for the
venue, free for the audience, and nearly free for you.

## The five-minute quick start

If you read nothing else today, read this and the two linked pages:

1. Skim [Pitch the Visit](01-pitch-the-visit.md) -- why staff say yes,
   and the one-page pitch that earns it.
2. Copy the [pitch letter](../templates/pitch-letter-template.md) and
   fill in the bracketed slots for a venue you actually like.
3. Keep [Plan the Fifty Minutes](02-plan-the-fifty-minutes.md) open as
   your session design from now on; that is the shape of the evening.

Everything else can wait until an email lands back marked "interested."

## How the manual is organized

The manual has two halves, and they are honestly different jobs.

- **The ask** (chapter 1): getting the yes without begging and without
  pretending you are someone else.
- **The room** (chapters 2-5): the fifty-minute shape of the session,
  large-print and plain-English accessibility rules, A/V and tables
  basics, and the book table that lets people buy with dignity and
  lets people *not* buy with dignity too.
- **The aftermath** (chapters 6-7): thanking the host so the venue
  remembers you fondly, and packing everything into a repeatable kit.

Templates are in a separate folder and are meant to be copied, not
admired. The glossary is plain English and safe to read on the way.

## Conventions used in the manual

- **Bracketed slots.** `[YOUR NAME]`, `[VENUE]` and friends mark places
  to personalize. Never send a template with brackets in it.
- **Checklists are real checkboxes.** The
  [day-of checklist](../templates/day-of-checklist.md) uses task boxes
  you can tick off on paper or on screen.
- **Everything is local-first.** The kit is folders on your computer
  and a box in your trunk. No service to subscribe to, no secret to
  enter, no data to upload.

## What this handbook is *not*

It is deliberately distinct from its two sibling handbooks, so nobody
wastes a chapter on overlap:

- *author-newsletter-handbook* covers what happens between visits: the
  reader email list, welcome sequences, and archiving. Visits and
  newsletters feed each other -- mention your signup at the visit, and
  read that handbook on the drive home -- but the tools live there.
- *kickstarter-campaign-handbook* covers crowdfunding a book. A library
  funds its shelves with taxes and a bookstore pays its rent with
  margin; ask neither to *back* you. They host, you deliver.

## One belief before chapter 1

A local visit is not a mini book conference. It is a neighbor doing a
show-and-tell of a thing they made, deep enough to respect the
audience's Tuesday evening, light enough that the library assistant who
is also there after work does not regret staying. If you keep believing
that, everything in this manual will feel less like stagecraft and
more like hospitality -- which is what it is.
