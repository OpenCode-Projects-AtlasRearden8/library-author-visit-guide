# Sell With Dignity

Nobody drives to a library to be sold at. But plenty of people will
gladly pay for a book they just watched you bring to life -- if the
buying is easy, priced, and completely free of pressure. Selling with
dignity means honoring both the guest who buys and the guest who
takes a bookmark and leaves; the second guest is a future reader, not
a missed transaction. This chapter is the table, the scripts, and the
lines you will not cross.

## First principles

- **Offer, never press.** Your job is to make buying possible and then
  stop. Persuasion past the first offer is not hospitality.
- **One table, visible prices, two payment methods.** Friction --
  not price -- is what kills small sales.
- **The free path is a real path.** Handouts, the library's own
  copies, and your free newsletter exist for people who are genuinely
  not buyers tonight. Market them warmly.
- **You are a guest, then a seller.** The hosting relationship with
  the library, store, or center matters more than one evening's
  receipts. Bookstores have house rules; obey before being told.

## The table setup, minute by minute

With the display from the [A/V and tables chapter](04-av-and-tables.md)
in place:

- books face-out in one row, prices large on a card -- never buried
  in conversation;
- a card reader plus a cash box with change for a ten and a five;
- bookmarks or flyers: title, the free monthly newsletter, a short
  line about the visit tonight;
- your signup sheet, on a clipboard, with a pen on a string;
- one sign reads "Take a bookmark -- free," so the no-pressure path is
  visible from across the room.

## Scripts that keep the selling kind

Words that work at the table (borrow them whole):

- Opening, when someone picks up the book: "Feel free to skim -- it's
  the table of contents page most people want to see first."
- The one soft ask, once: "I'll be here through eight o'clock if you
  decide you'd like a signed copy."
- For browsers who put it down: "No worries at all -- the handout has
  my reading-club page and where the library keeps copies."
- When a guest asks what to buy someone else: give a genuine answer,
  even when the answer is another book. Dignity compounds.

Words that are banned from your mouth forever:

- "It's for charity" (unless it truly is) and any urgency you invented.
- "You can't get this anywhere else" when the library two blocks away
  has it on the shelf, which you checked.
- Anything that follows a guest who walked away. Never.

## Signed copies, done well

Ask three things: the name's spelling, whether it is for them or a
gift, and one fact about the recipient if it is for a gift -- a single
personalized line in the signing ("For Mei -- who asked the stubborn
question") is what turns a sale into a story. Use pencil first if you
fear misspelling names; guests forgive pencil drafts in ink, not ink
that is wrong.

## Store and venue rules, stated up front

Different venues sell on different rails:

- **Bookstore:** the house almost always handles the till -- you sign,
  the store sells, they keep their margin, and you earn royalty on
  their wholesale terms or accept theirs for consignment. Ask one
  question before the event: "Do you ring books up at the counter and
  I just sign, or do you want consignment paperwork?" Ask it days
  early, never while a guest is standing there.
- **Library and community centers:** usually your table, your reader,
  your receipts. Public institutions sometimes have rules about sales
  on premises, so ask "is selling allowed, or should I take signups
  and honor them afterward?" Both options are fine.
- **Either way:** post-visit, send the host a two-line recap
  (attendance, copies sold) and thank them -- see
  [Thank the Host](06-thank-the-host.md).

## The receipts, the strings, the law, kept friendly

- **Keep a simple sales ledger:** date, venue, units, gross, fees.
  One sheet, updated the same night, is all bookkeeping this needs.
- **Local rules:** some institutions require you to collect or remit
  sales tax; check your state once, write down the answer, and put the
  form or number in the visit file. Not tax advice -- just a reminder
  that it exists.
- **The counts travel with you.** Chapter 7's kit includes a stock
  card: how many copies left home with you for each event. Nothing
  sours a repeat invitation like a wrong tally.

## The dignity test

Guests leaving the event should feel one of two good ways: glad they
bought, or glad they did not have to. If a guest felt trapped, the
dignity failed, whatever the number of sales. Run that audit after
every visit; the honest ones tell you something every time.
