# Plan the Fifty Minutes

Everything awkward about author events is a plan problem. When an
event drags, it is usually because nobody drew a map of the hour. This
chapter is that map: a fifty-minute session that mixes reading,
questions, and a low-key demonstration of your tools, with buffers a
room of strangers actually needs. Print it, hand it to a nervous
volunteer, run the night from it. The fifty minutes are sketched here
down to the minute, and the [day-of checklist](../templates/day-of-checklist.md)
turns the sketch into boxes you tick.

## The arc on one page

| Minutes | Segment | What it is | Why it is there |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0-5 | Welcome and settle | Greet, say what the hour looks like, invite people to move chairs | Strangers need orientation before they can listen |
| 5-13 | The reading | One rehearsed passage, told aloud, eight minutes max | Proof the book itself is good |
| 13-18 | The five-minute story | How you became a person who made this book -- the nurse-to-author story, the real reason | The pivot from product to person |
| 18-30 | The low-key tool demo | Watch you use your tools for ten unhurried minutes | For how-to authors: show the making, not a webinar |
| 30-38 | First question round | Two or three eager early questions | Opens airflow before the Q&A free-for-all |
| 38-47 | Open Q&A | The whole room, moderated | Where people decide you are worth following |
| 47-50 | Close, thanks, table time | Thank the crowd and staff, point at the table, sign and chat | Ends punctual; sales happen here naturally |

Add it up and notice the shape: the audience hears your voice for
eighteen minutes, then the tools in a low-key form, and by minute 38
the evening is mostly their hand. That ratio -- author-led first,
audience-led second -- is what people later describe as "such a nice
event."

## The reading: pick, rehearse, land

- **Pick a scene with an ending, not a monologue.** Choose a passage
  that closes a small loop -- a decision, a laugh, a turn -- so people
  hear a full shape, not the middle of an argument.
- **Aim for 900-1,100 words.** At a comfortable reading pace that is
  roughly eight minutes. Time it twice and accept the worse time.
- **Mark your copy in high-contrast ink:** breath slashes for runs,
  "SLOW" over names, a big "STOP" at the end. Read from a printed page,
  14-point or larger, even if you know it by heart.
- **Read slightly slower than feels natural.** Rooms eat faster than
  you think and ears have no rewind.

## The five-minute story

Between the book and the demo, tell how the book happened. Two or three
moves, first person, no slides:

- the moment the book became necessary to you,
- the one thing you needed that nobody explained plainly,
- what changed once you understood it -- the promise the book keeps.

Nurses are allowed to say "I was a nurse and tired of explaining this
badly." That sentence alone does more trust-building than any bio.

## The tool demo, at reception-party volume

You are not a conference speaker and the room knows it; keep the demo
kind:

- **One tool, one task, ten minutes.** Show the thing you actually do --
  drafting a chapter outline, generating alt text for an image, turning
  messy notes into a plain-English page -- start, narrate, finish.
  Finished beats impressive.
- **Narrate what the audience cannot see.** "On my screen you're seeing
  the calendar heat map" is what keeps back-row eyes included.
- **Airplane-mode safety net.** Prepare so the demo survives no Wi-Fi:
  a local model or pre-saved screenshots of the same steps on a backup
  stick. Announce your backup should the network drop; that alone never
  looks like failure. And bring the printed output from the last real
  run of that tool, so the results are in guests' hands either way.
- **Invite one volunteer to drive.** For twenty seconds, a guest clicks
  the "send" key. The room relaxes -- nothing mystical is happening.

## The Q&A: airflow before abundance

- **Planted questions.** Ask one kind soul (a friend, an earlier
  arrival) to ask the first question if silence stretches. It sounds
  old-fashioned and it works.
- **Repeat every question into the room.** For hearing accessibility
  and for the recording-free path: "The question was, 'How much does it
  cost a month?'" Then answer it to the whole room, not the asker.
- **See chapter 3.** Repeat all questions and use the microphone the
  host offers, always.
- **Politely cap.** "Two more good ones" keeps the night punctual, and
  punctuality is kindness.

## Close the room like a good host

- Thank the audience, name the staff by first name, point at the book
  table and the free handouts.
- End on time even mid-thought: the discipline says more than the
  thought would.
- Stay at the table for another fifteen minutes while chairs get put
  away by someone who is not you -- offer anyway.

## If the slot is shorter

Venues hand you forty minutes or an hour without notice. Cut from the
ends, not the middle. With a 40-minute slot: trim the welcome and the
second question round. With an hour: no, do not add slides -- widen Q&A
and table time instead. The spine stays identical.
